1 · Amplitudes and their square

A qubit is written as |ψ⟩ = α·|0⟩ + β·|1⟩. The two numbers α and β are called amplitudes. They are not probabilities — you only get those by squaring.

P(0) = |α|²  ·  P(1) = |β|²
Born rule — the bridge from calculation to measurement

Since every measurement gives exactly one result, the two probabilities must add up to 1. This is the normalisation:

|α|² + |β|² = 1
Every allowed qubit state satisfies it

Calculating forwards

Given |ψ⟩ = 0.6·|0⟩ + 0.8·|1⟩:

  1. P(0) = 0.6² = 0.36 — that is 36 %
  2. P(1) = 0.8² = 0.64 — that is 64 %
  3. Check: 0.36 + 0.64 = 1 ✓

The most common trap: reading 0.8 as “80 %”. Only the square is the probability.

Calculating backwards

Given |ψ⟩ = 0.6·|0⟩ + b·|1⟩ with real b > 0. Find b:

  1. Set up the normalisation: 0.6² + b² = 1
  2. Rearrange: b² = 1 − 0.36 = 0.64
  3. Take the square root: b = 0.8

2 · Why 2n changes everything

A single qubit needs two amplitudes: one for |0⟩, one for |1⟩. Two qubits have four possible results — |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩ — and therefore need four amplitudes. With every additional qubit the number doubles.

n qubits → 2n amplitudes
Together they describe the state completely
QubitsAmplitudesOrder of magnitude
12trivial

3002³⁰⁰more than there are atoms in the observable universe

That is exactly why a larger quantum computer can no longer be simulated on classical computers — not because it would be “faster”, but because the bookkeeping explodes.

3 · What a measurement does

Before the measurement a qubit is in superposition. The measurement gives exactly one result, and afterwards the state really is that result — the superposition is gone. This is called collapse.

What follows from this
If you measure (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and get 1, the qubit is now in |1⟩. An immediate second measurement gives 1 again — with certainty, no longer by chance. The randomness lies in the first look, not the second.

Superposition is not ignorance

With a covered coin, the result has long been fixed; we just don't know it. With a qubit it is not fixed. The difference is measurable: only genuine superpositions can overlap and cancel each other out. Mere ignorance can never do that — where nothing oscillates, nothing cancels.

The test with the Hadamard gate is based on exactly this: applying H twice in a row brings |0⟩ back to exactly |0⟩. If the intermediate state had only been an unknown coin toss, 1 would come out in half of the cases.

No consciousness required

“Measuring” means an interaction that carries information to the outside — a detector, a photographic plate, a molecule of air. No person needs to look. A detector measures at night too, when nobody is in the lab. That large objects never appear in superposition has the same cause: they constantly interact with their surroundings — decoherence.

4 · Gates are rotations

Every qubit state is a point on the Bloch sphere. The angle θ measures how far the state is from the north pole |0⟩. A gate rotates this point — nothing more.

P(1) = sin²(θ/2)
Watch out for the half θ — that's why the north and south poles are 180° apart
θP(1)State
0°0|0⟩ — north pole
60°0,25sin²(30°) = 0.25
90°0,5Equator, e.g. |+⟩
120°0,75sin²(60°) = 0.75
180°1|1⟩ — south pole

A rotation Ry(φ) about the y-axis increases θ directly by φ. So if you start at |0⟩ (θ = 0°) and apply Ry(120°), you end up at θ = 120° — and therefore at P(1) = 0.75.

The four you need

H Hadamard. Brings |0⟩ to the equator and creates the equal superposition (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 — so P(0) = P(1) = 0.5. The standard tool for superposition.
H·H Applying H twice cancels out: back to exactly |0⟩.
X Half a turn about x. Turns |0⟩ into |1⟩ — the NOT gate of the quantum world.
Z Half a turn about z. The poles |0⟩ and |1⟩ stay put, but on the equator everything turns to the opposite side: |+⟩ → |−⟩.
Why Z of all gates flips |+⟩
|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 lies on the equator. Z flips the sign in front of |1⟩ and turns it into |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2 — the opposite point. Measured in the 0/1 basis nothing changes (both give 50 : 50). The difference only becomes visible when you apply H again afterwards: |+⟩ becomes |0⟩, |−⟩ becomes |1⟩.

5 · How much energy is in light?

Light comes in portions — photons. The energy of one portion depends only on the frequency, not on the brightness. Brighter light means more photons, not stronger ones.

E = h·f = h·c / λ
h = 6.626·10⁻³⁴ J·s — Planck's constant

In practice nobody calculates with these powers of ten. Measure energy in electronvolts and wavelength in nanometres, and the formula shrinks to a number you can remember:

E [eV] = 1240 / λ [nm]
h·c ≈ 1240 eV·nm — the most useful rule of thumb in optics

Example

What is the energy of a photon with λ = 620 nm?

  1. Set up the formula: E = 1240 / 620
  2. Calculate: E = 2.0 eV
  3. Sanity check: 620 nm is orange-red — the low-energy end of visible light

Memory aid: short wavelength, high energy. That's why UV light gives you sunburn and red light doesn't.

6 · The Bell state

Two qubits can be linked in such a way that neither has a state of its own — only the pair together. The best-known such state:

|Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩) / √2
Built from H on the first qubit, then CNOT

The bracket contains only |00⟩ and |11⟩. Whatever isn't there has amplitude 0 — and by the Born rule therefore probability 0:

ResultAmplitudeProbability
001/√250 %
0100 % — never occurs
1000 % — never occurs
111/√250 %

So if you measure the first qubit and get 0, the second one gives 0 as well, with certainty — no matter how far apart the two are. Seen on its own, each of the two qubits is still completely random: 50 to 50.

And why you can't send signals faster than light with it
You can't choose your result — it comes out at random. So you can't put a message into it. Anyone looking only at their own side sees pure noise. The agreement only becomes visible when both sides compare their lists — and that comparison goes through an ordinary channel, so at most at the speed of light.

What it is already used for today

The tangible use is eavesdropping-proof key exchange, quantum cryptography. Two parties share entangled pairs and obtain a common random sequence from them as a secret key. An eavesdropper would have to measure — and every measurement detectably destroys the state. So the attack gives itself away instead of going unnoticed. Such links are already running between cities and via satellite.

7 · What quantum technology can do — and what it can't

Quantum physics is not exotic

It doesn't only apply to the tiniest things in the lab. Lasers, LEDs, solar cells, the transistors in every phone chip, MRI in hospitals and the atomic clocks in GPS all rely on it. It is the best-confirmed theory in physics — and it's in your pocket.

A quantum computer does not try everything

The most widespread misconception. All possibilities are in play at the same time — but you may only measure once, and the answer would come out at random. It only becomes useful if you first cancel out the wrong possibilities through interference and reinforce the right one. That's exactly where most ideas for quantum algorithms fail.

And it won't replace your phone either

A quantum computer is a specialist tool, not a better all-rounder: superior at certain tasks, slower than your laptop at most others. For videos, chats and games, a normal computer stays clearly ahead. On top of that comes the effort — cooling almost to absolute zero and heavy shielding, because every disturbance destroys the superposition. The most promising use is simulating molecules and materials: they are quantum systems themselves.

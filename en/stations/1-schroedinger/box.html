In front of you are two wooden boxes, each with four combination locks. They are not a quantum experiment and don't pretend to be one. They are the goal of the puzzles, and what's inside is yours.

The story of the cat

The story begins with an exchange of letters. In the summer of 1935, Albert Einstein and Erwin Schrödinger write to each other, and both are unhappy with the new quantum theory. Einstein comes up with a keg of gunpowder that, according to the rules of the theory, both explodes and does not explode. Schrödinger likes this picture so much that later that year he looks for an example everyone can picture immediately — and he picks a cat.

The setup, as he describes it
A cat sits in a steel chamber, and next to it is a Geiger counter with such a tiny amount of radioactive substance that within an hour perhaps one single atom decays — and perhaps none at all. If one decays, the counter clicks and releases a hammer that shatters a small flask of poison. Nobody is watching.

After an hour the atom has decayed and not decayed, and since the counter depends on the atom, the hammer on the counter and the cat on the hammer, in the end the cat is both as well — if you follow the calculation consistently. This is exactly where Schrödinger drew the line, because his thought experiment was not a claim, but an objection.

The image became very famous afterwards, but it soon stood for exactly the opposite, and the criticism turned into the mascot of quantum physics. Still, Schrödinger was half right: single particles are measurably in superposition, but a cat never would be. The bigger an object, the faster its surroundings destroy this state, and this process is called decoherence.

And what about our boxes?

Our boxes are plain wooden crates, each with four combination locks. Box 1 is the easier one, Box 2 the harder one. Each puzzle gives you a four-digit code, and each code opens exactly one of the locks. Only when all four are open does the lid come off.

Inside there is no physics, but a small reward to take home. We have put in a few souvenirs of this day, and whoever gets the box open may choose one. We won't give away more than that here.

Please lock it again
Take your souvenir, then close the box again and scramble its four locks. That way the next people get the same fun cracking it as you did.

Why not a real quantum box?

Because you can't build superposition out of wood and combination locks. There are three reasons behind this, and all three are worth a look.

1. Nothing is really undecided

In a wooden box, what's inside is always already fixed, even if you don't know it yet. In a real quantum system, by contrast, there is no hidden value before the measurement — and that is the crucial difference.

2. No real interference

The most exciting part of quantum mechanics is that possibilities can cancel each other out. For that you need waves, and you can try them out yourself in puzzle 2 and puzzle 3.

3. Size makes the difference

A real cat would never be in superposition. The more particles an object has, the faster its surroundings destroy the state, and for everyday things that takes unimaginably tiny fractions of a second. This process is called decoherence.

So why a box at all?

Real quantum experiments need vacuum, lasers and often temperatures close to absolute zero — hard to come by on a lawn in front of the university.

So the physics is not in the boxes, but in the puzzles in front of them. The boxes are the reason to solve them, and the souvenir inside is the memory of it.

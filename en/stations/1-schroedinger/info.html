Background

What is
superposition?

The shortest honest answer: a quantum object doesn't have to decide — until we measure it.

The coin that is still in the air

A coin in the air is a vivid picture of superposition — but only with one important caveat: while it is flying, you could say the outcome is still open. As soon as it lands, we see heads or tails.

In classical physics, however, the outcome is fixed the whole time. If we knew everything about its speed, spin and the air currents, we could in principle predict how the coin will land. We just don't know the answer yet.

A quantum particle is different. Before a measurement it is not simply in a state whose value we don't know. It can genuinely be in a superposition of several possible states — put simply: not “either A or B”, but a quantum-mechanical combination of A and B.

Only a measurement forces the system to give a definite result.

So the coin is only a metaphor. With the coin, we are missing knowledge. In a quantum system, the indeterminacy itself is part of physical reality.

The difference in one sentence
Ignorance means: the answer is fixed, I just don't know it.
Superposition means: the answer is not fixed at all yet.

The notation

The puzzles use a notation that may be new to you:

|0⟩ One definite basis state. Comparable to the classical 0. Shown as a colour, for example: blue.
|1⟩ The other definite basis state. Comparable to the classical 1. Shown as a colour, for example: red.
(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 Both with equal probability — the superposition.

The notation with a vertical bar and an angle bracket, |…⟩, is called a ket and goes back to Paul Dirac. It simply means: “this is a quantum state”. When measured, the superposition collapses either into |0⟩ or into |1⟩.

And why would anyone care?

Because it turns into technology. A classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit (a quantum bit) can be in superposition — and with every additional qubit, the space of possibilities in play at the same time doubles.

The catch: when you measure, you still get only one result in the end. The whole art of quantum computing is to let the possibilities overlap beforehand in such a way that the wrong ones cancel each other out — and the right one remains. That is exactly interference, and it is what puzzles 2 and 3 are about.

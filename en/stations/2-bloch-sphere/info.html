Background

What is
a qubit?

The shortest answer: the smallest possible quantum system — one with exactly two possible measurement results.

Step 1: the bit

A classical bit is a light switch. Off or on. 0 or 1. There is nothing in between. Your whole phone is made of billions of such switches.

Step 2: the dimmer

At first a qubit looks like a dimmer: it can be set anywhere between 0 and 1. But careful — the picture is misleading.

When you measure a qubit, you never get “a little bit of 0”. You get 0 or 1, clearly, just like with the switch. What the dimmer sets are only the probabilities.

The crux
A qubit doesn't store “0.7”. It stores a tendency to give 0 or 1 when measured — and this tendency can overlap with other tendencies and cancel out. No dimmer can do that.

Step 3: the second number

This is where it gets interesting. One number is not enough to describe a qubit. It takes two:

θ theta How strongly does it lean towards 0 or 1? → the latitude
φ phi Which phase does it have? → the longitude

Two angles that fix a direction — that is exactly the description of a point on the surface of a sphere. And there you have the Bloch sphere. It is not a metaphor, but the exact map of all the states a qubit can have.

What is the phase?

Think of a wave. Two waves can be equally high and still be shifted — one crest comes earlier than the other. This shift is the phase.

In a single measurement you don't see any of it. But as soon as two paths come together again, the phase decides everything: crest on crest reinforces, crest on trough cancels. A quantum computer works with exactly this — it sets the phases so that the wrong answers wipe each other out.

And what's useful about it?

A single qubit isn't impressive yet. The leap comes when you connect them: with n qubits the description needs 2n numbers. With 300 qubits that's more numbers than there are atoms in the observable universe.

The catch remains: in the end you may only measure once and you get n bits out. The art is to superpose so cleverly beforehand that precisely the answer you're looking for remains. That's why quantum computers are not “faster computers”, but specialists for very particular tasks.

To be honest
The Bloch sphere only works for a single qubit. For two there is no longer a picture you could draw — and that is exactly where entanglement lives. In physics, nice pictures often have an expiry date.

All eight gates at a glance

X Half a turn about the x-axis. Turns 0 into 1 — the NOT gate of the quantum world.
Y Half a turn about y. Also swaps the poles, but with a different phase.
Z Half a turn about z. The poles stay — only the phase flips.
H Hadamard. Brings |0⟩ to the equator: creates superposition. The most important gate of all.
S Quarter turn about z (90°). Turns |+⟩ to the y-pole: |+i⟩.
S† S backwards (−90° about z). The inverse rotation of S. Turns |+⟩ to |−i⟩.
T Eighth of a turn about z (45°). The finest common phase screw — applying T twice gives exactly S.
T† T backwards (−45° about z). The inverse of T.

And any angle at all?

X, Y, Z, S, S†, T and T† are all just special cases of a single tool: a rotation about an axis by a freely chosen angle. These have their own names:

Rx(θ) Rotation by θ degrees about the x-axis (through |+⟩ and |−⟩).
Ry(θ) Rotation by θ degrees about the y-axis (through |+i⟩ and |−i⟩).
Rz(θ) Rotation by θ degrees about the z-axis (through |0⟩ and |1⟩).

On the model this means: you simply hold the sphere so that the named axis stays fixed, and swing it by the given angle. The direction of rotation follows the right-hand rule: the thumb points in the positive direction of the axis (for z upwards to |0⟩, for x to |+⟩, for y to |+i⟩), and the curled fingers give the direction of rotation for positive angles. So S = Rz(90°) turns the state |+⟩ into |+i⟩.

This makes X = Rx(180°), Y = Ry(180°), Z = Rz(180°) — and S = Rz(90°), S† = Rz(−90°), T = Rz(45°), T† = Rz(−45°). Everything that happens on the sphere is some combination of these three rotations.

Watch out
After measuring, the arrow jumps to a pole — and stays there. The previous position is irretrievably gone. That is exactly what “the measurement destroys the superposition” means.
Try this
Tap H, then H once more. You end up exactly at |0⟩ again. The same half turn twice cancels itself out — a nice example of the fact that every gate is reversible.
Why doesn't φ change the probabilities?
Because φ is the phase. In a single measurement in the 0/1 direction it is completely invisible. It only matters when two paths meet again — then it decides whether something is reinforced or cancelled. That is exactly puzzle 3.
Why does every gate have to be reversible?
Because a rotation never destroys information — you can turn it back. A classical AND gate can't do that: from a 0 at the output you can't reconstruct what went in. Quantum gates are always reversible. Only the measurement isn't, and that's why it comes at the very end.
And where is entanglement?
For that you need gates that act on two qubits at once — such as CNOT. That can't be shown on a single sphere. At the QuantumTable you can plug together exactly such circuits yourself.
Is the randomness of the measurement real?
Not here on the page — your browser rolls the dice with a pseudo-random number generator. In nature, yes: as far as we know, the outcome of a quantum measurement is fundamentally unpredictable, not just in practice.

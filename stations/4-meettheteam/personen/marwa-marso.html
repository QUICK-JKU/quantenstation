How much can we learn from a glimpse?

Field Quantum Computing

What I do

I'm a PhD student and like to think of my research as an attempt to answer an old question: how much can we learn about the world around us, and how do we do it as efficiently as possible? In my case, the world I study is quantum, and quantum information behaves in ways that everyday, classical information does not.

A single measurement only gives a small, random piece of information about a quantum state, and disturbs the state in the process. So learning anything reliable takes many repeated copies, and the real question becomes how to use them as effectively as possible. The usual approach is to measure each copy the moment you get it, turning it into a simple classical number. This works remarkably well, but it also throws something away: once a copy is measured, it is gone, and you can never ask a question that involves it together with the next one. Looking at two copies together lets you ask a different kind of question, one about the relationship between them. Two copies held together form a new object, with new questions neither copy alone could answer.

My work explores exactly this: how many copies you need to hold together before a given property of the state becomes learnable at all, and how that number decides what is possible versus practically out of reach. Sometimes that gap is the difference between an experiment nobody could ever run and one that is entirely practical.

Why it matters

Somewhere between the quantum and the classical, there is a boundary, a moment where the strange possibilities of a quantum state collapse into an ordinary thing. That boundary is so intriguing to me, and I would like to understand exactly what happens there. What we can know or what is simply not in our reach.

I also love that something this deep does not have to stay locked away. Quantum mechanics can feel intimidating, but the questions underneath it are questions anyone can hold: how much can we ever really know about something we cannot directly see? And because quantum computing is still such a young field, we get to help shape what it becomes, not just scientifically, but in who feels welcome to take part.
